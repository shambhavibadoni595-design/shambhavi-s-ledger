A ledger everyone keeps together.

For most of history, records of who-owns-what were kept by one trusted party — a bank, a registry office, a platform. Web3 asks a stranger question: what if the ledger itself was the trusted party, copied everywhere, owned nowhere?

001

What a blockchain actually is

Strip away the hype and a blockchain is a record book copied across thousands of computers instead of locked in one office. Each new page — a block — lists a batch of transactions and is sealed with a hash: a short fingerprint generated from everything on that page.

Here's the clever part: each block's hash also depends on the hash of the block before it. Change one word on an old page and its fingerprint changes, which breaks every fingerprint after it. That's what "immutable" really means here — not impossible to alter, just instantly obvious and expensive to fake.

002

Decentralization: who keeps the copy

A normal ledger has one keeper. If the bank makes an error, or the platform shuts down, the record goes with it. A blockchain hands a copy to everyone in the network and settles disagreements through consensus — rules like Proof of Work or Proof of Stake that decide whose version of the next block gets accepted by the rest.

003

Cryptocurrency: entries, not coins

A coin like Bitcoin or Ether is really a line in the shared ledger saying "this much value moved from address A to address B." Your wallet doesn't hold coins the way a leather wallet holds cash — it holds a private key, a secret signature proving the entries belong to you.

Lose the key and you lose access, permanently. There is no branch to walk into and no password-reset email — which is exactly the trade-off decentralization makes.

004

Smart contracts: rules that run themselves

A smart contract writes "if this, then that" directly into the ledger. If address A sends 1 ETH, release the ticket. If the auction closes, send the funds to the highest bidder. Once deployed, nobody can pull it aside for a favor — it executes exactly as written, every time.

It resembles a vending machine more than a legal contract: no clerk, no landlord, no discretion. That reliability is the entire pitch, and also the entire risk — a bug written into the machine runs just as faithfully as a correct one.

005

NFTs: unique entries in a fungible world

One Bitcoin is worth exactly one Bitcoin, always — that's what "fungible" means. An NFT (non-fungible token) is a ledger entry that instead says: this one thing is unique, and this address owns it — closer to a deed or a ticket stub than to money.

What's provably owned is often just the deed, not the file itself — the artwork usually lives elsewhere, with the token pointing to it.

006

DAOs: bylaws written as code

A DAO — Decentralized Autonomous Organization — runs a group the way a smart contract runs a transaction: budgets and voting rules live in code, not a binder in a filing cabinet. Members hold tokens that double as voting weight, and proposals pass or fail by the ledger's own tally.

No CEO signs off. The organization's "decision" is whatever the code and the vote produce — for better and for worse.
